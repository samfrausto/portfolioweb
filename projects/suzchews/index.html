The Problem & The Solution ACAD 432 · Spring 2026

Dysgeusia

A taste disorder that turns scrambled eggs into taste — and a gum designed to give it back.

Two of the most important people in my life are cancer survivors. What I didn't expect — and what neither of them did either — was that the part of treatment that hurt most wasn't the fatigue or the nausea. It was the food. Coffee tasted like metal. Scrambled eggs tasted like rancid butter. A bowl of soup, the one thing they could keep down some days, came out of the microwave tasting like nothing at all.

That symptom has a name: dysgeusia. It is one of the most common, most under-discussed, and most under-addressed side effects of chemotherapy.

“I knew I would feel horrible from the chemo, but I thought I'd at least be able to taste my scrambled eggs in the morning.”

— overheard, Spring 2024
17%
of the entire US population experience dysgeusia at some point in their life. For chemotherapy patients specifically, prevalence ranges from 17 to 86%. Source: Cleveland Clinic; literature review §02

A note on terminology

Dysgeusia is the clinical term for distorted taste — food perceived as metallic, bitter, rancid, or simply absent. It is distinct from ageusia (loss of taste entirely) and hypogeusia (reduced taste). All three commonly co-occur in cancer patients.

Introducing SuzChews.

A chewing gum — the first ever brand designed for cancer patients — built around four ingredient strategies that work together to restore the taste experience while a patient is on treatment.
