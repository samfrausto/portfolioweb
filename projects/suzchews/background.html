§02 — Background & Rationale SUZCHEWS / 2026.05 / 02

Why dysgeusia is the right problem to solve.

Dysgeusia — the distortion of taste — affects between 17 and 86 percent of cancer patients on active treatment. The mechanism is multi-causal: chemotherapeutic agents damage taste bud receptor cells, alter salivary chemistry, bind metal ions to the tongue, and produce reactive carbonyls through oral epithelial lipid peroxidation. The time course is variable but well-bounded. No mouth-resident, continuously stimulating intervention exists today. This section establishes those facts and the gap they reveal.

§ 02.1

The landscape.

Dysgeusia is one of the most under-discussed major side effects of chemotherapy. Prevalence estimates in the literature range from 17 percent to 86 percent of patients on active treatment 1 2. That seven-fold range is itself revealing: it reflects how differently dysgeusia presents across drug classes, treatment regimens, and patient populations — not noise in the data.

The standard instrument for measuring it is the CiTAS scale (Chemotherapy-induced Taste Alteration Scale) 3: eighteen items, five-point Likert, three dimensions covering quantitative changes in flavor perception (hypogeusia, ageusia), qualitative changes (heterogeusia, cacogeusia), and nutrition-related effects 4. CiTAS is the closest the field has to a shared vocabulary, and it is what §05 of this deliverable uses to map symptom profiles to formulation strategies.

Methodology note

The seven-fold prevalence range is partly an artifact of how dysgeusia gets measured: subjective scales (CiTAS), objective psychophysical testing (taste-strip), and chart-review of "complaint" notes give meaningfully different numbers.

CiTAS dimensions

I. Quantitative: hypogeusia, ageusia. II. Qualitative: heterogeusia, cacogeusia. III. Nutrition-related: difficulty with hot/fat foods.

§ 02.2

The mechanism.

Dysgeusia is not a single failure mode. Four mechanistically distinct injuries co-occur during chemotherapy, each contributing to the overall taste disturbance:

Taste bud receptor cell apoptosis

Taste bud cells turn over rapidly under normal physiology. Cytotoxic chemotherapy agents accelerate apoptosis without proportional regeneration, leaving fewer functional receptors to detect tastants. The effect is most pronounced for salt, which is consistently the most compromised primary taste in post-treatment recovery 5.

Salivary chemistry shift

Saliva is the medium that solubilizes tastants for receptor contact. Chemotherapy alters salivary pH, protein composition, and buffering capacity. Counter-intuitively, Jensen et al. found no statistically significant relationship between chemotherapy-induced taste alterations and salivary flow rate per se 6 — the chemistry of saliva matters more than the volume.

Direct metal-ion binding

Several agents — particularly the platinum-based class (cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin) and iron-containing supplements — bind metal ions directly to the tongue surface, producing the characteristic metallic taste that many patients describe. Iron-rich foods (red meat especially) can worsen the sensation 7.

Reactive carbonyl production via lipid peroxidation

Oral epithelial cells under oxidative stress from chemotherapy undergo lipid peroxidation, producing reactive carbonyl compounds. These compounds contribute to the bitter and rancid quality of dysgeusia in a way that is mechanistically separate from the receptor-cell loss, and they are why simply "stimulating" remaining taste buds is insufficient — the molecules themselves are generating bad signal at the chemoreceptor level 8.

A fifth contributor — peripheral and central neuropathy with cranial-nerve neurotransmission blockage — appears in some regimens and adds a layer that pharmacological taste interventions cannot easily reach 9. Section §04 addresses how the gum strategy targets the four reachable pathways and explicitly acknowledges what it cannot reach.

Why apoptosis matters

Taste bud cells normally regenerate every 10–14 days. Chemo skews the rate of death-to-renewal, so symptoms peak after first exposure and recover slowly post-treatment.

A surprising negative

The Jensen et al. finding — that salivary flow rate is uncorrelated with dysgeusia — reframes a popular intervention idea (artificial saliva). The chemistry, not the volume, is the lever.

Platinum agents

Cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin. These are the regimens most commonly associated with metallic taste.

§ 02.3

The time course.

The clinical implication of Fig. 02.1 is that dysgeusia has a well-bounded intervention window. Symptoms emerge predictably after first exposure, escalate over weeks, and recover slowly post-treatment. Any intervention designed to address the experience — rather than the underlying disease — needs to be (a) deployable within days of first exposure, (b) tolerable across weeks-to-months, and (c) usable through the long tail of post-treatment recovery.

That requirement set excludes most pharmacological options (which add burden to an already over-medicated patient) and most dietary modifications (which patients lack the energy to maintain). It strongly favors a passive, palatable, mouth-resident intervention — which is the design thesis behind SuzChews.

Why salt is the slowest to recover

Sodium-channel taste receptors (ENaC) sit on a subpopulation of taste cells with longer regeneration cycles. They are also disproportionately affected by salivary buffering shifts.

Treatment-severity caveat

Anthracyclines, paclitaxel, carboplatin, and docetaxel produce the highest dysgeusia rates 5. Cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil are less likely to cause taste alterations, despite cisplatin's metallic-binding profile — mechanism is not the only factor.

§ 02.4

The variability problem.

The seven-fold prevalence range from §02.1 is the first hint that dysgeusia is not one disorder — it is a family of presentations. Two patients on the same regimen at the same week of treatment can describe wildly different experiences: one tastes everything as bitter, one loses salt entirely, one experiences metallic intensity that fluctuates by hour.

That variability has consequences for any intervention strategy. An aggregate "dysgeusia gum" that targets the average patient will work for some and fail for many. The right design is one that decomposes the symptom space, identifies which mechanisms are dominant for a given patient, and recommends an ingredient mix matched to that patient. That decomposition is what §05 builds. The simulation in §06 lets a reader experience the variability directly.

Variables that drive variation

Treatment regimen, oral hygiene, age, iron and zinc levels, prior taste-altering disease, weeks since first cycle. §05 weights these into a per-patient formulation.

Variables we explored and ruled out

Two candidate variables — generic taste-altering disease history (unrelated comorbidities like reflux, diabetes) and chronological age in isolation — were considered and dropped from the algorithm in §05. Disease history was uncorrelated with dysgeusia severity in the literature once treatment regimen was controlled for 11; age was correlated with severity but the mechanism is mediated entirely through taste-bud-fragility, which is captured by the recovery-time variable in §05. Including both would have inflated dimensionality without adding signal.

§ 02.5

The gap.

Existing strategies for managing chemotherapy-induced dysgeusia fall into three buckets, none of which fit the requirement set developed above.

Dietary modification (use plastic utensils, marinate proteins, cold over hot food) places ongoing cognitive and physical load on a patient already operating below baseline. Pharmacological supplementation (zinc gluconate, glutamine, lactoferrin) has weak and inconsistent evidence and adds to medication burden. General-purpose candy and lozenges are convenient and palatable but were designed for halitosis or dry mouth, not for dysgeusia — they do not target the mechanisms in §02.2 and they do not address the salt-perception loss that defines post-treatment recovery.

SuzChews fills that gap. A chewing gum is mouth-resident, continuously stimulating, requires no behavioral change, and can be formulated to target the four reachable mechanisms simultaneously. The remainder of this site — §03 through §06 — documents what that gum is, how it was designed, and what the evidence and methodology behind it look like.

Existing interventions

Dietary advice, supplements (zinc, glutamine), and general-purpose candy. None designed for dysgeusia specifically.

Why a gum, specifically

Mouth-resident · continuously stimulating · no medication burden · palatable across nausea states · familiar form factor.
